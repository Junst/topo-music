\begin{abstract}
Probing studies establish that audio representations encode musical attributes
such as key, and this is routinely read as evidence that the representations are
musically organised. We show these are different claims. Measuring the same
$24$ musical keys on the same corpus three ways---linear decodability, the
geometry of class centroids, and the geometry of a linear readout's
weights---we find they dissociate: a log-CQT decodes key better than
MERT-v1-330M ($.492$ vs.\ $.456$) while its centroid geometry shows no
circle-of-fifths structure at all ($\rho=-0.035$), yet the same structure is
strongly present in its probe weights ($+0.560$). We then identify two distinct
mechanisms that hide musical structure from the ambient metric.
\emph{Sample-limited masking} is class-independent nuisance variance; it
averages away, and it alone accounts for clip-level key geometry
($13.4\times$ from centroid sample size against $1.7\times$ from a
$1000\times$ longer pooling window). \emph{Metric masking} is nuisance
that is a function of the class; it does not average away at any sample size,
and a rank-$4$ projection fitted on GiantSteps keys---never on notes---recovers
octave equivalence in individual NSynth recordings using ${<}0.3\%$ of the
variance. The distinction is not specific to music representations.
\end{abstract}

\begin{keywords}
music representation learning, probing, representation geometry, key detection,
octave equivalence
\end{keywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Self-supervised audio encoders support strong linear probes
\cite{probes} for key, pitch, genre and instrument \cite{mert,marble}, and the
usual inference is that the attribute is \emph{organised} in the
representation. Music makes that inference
unusually testable, because music theory specifies not just which classes exist
but how they are related: keys a perfect fifth apart share six of seven scale
degrees and sit adjacent on the circle of fifths \cite{krumhansl1982,
krumhansl1990}; pitches an octave apart are the same pitch class
\cite{shepard1982}. A representation can therefore encode key perfectly while
placing $\mathrm{C}$ and $\mathrm{G}$ no closer than $\mathrm{C}$ and
$\mathrm{F}\sharp$.

We ask when that theoretical structure is visible in the geometry a downstream
model actually consumes, and find that decodability, ambient geometry and
readout geometry come apart (Sec.~\ref{sec:three}). We then separate two
reasons a representation can contain musical structure that its metric does not
show. Writing an item of class $y$ as
\begin{equation}
z = s_y + n_y + \varepsilon, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid y] = 0,
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
with $s_y$ the structure of interest, $\varepsilon$ nuisance independent of the
class and $n_y$ nuisance that is a \emph{function} of the class, the two terms
behave differently under the two operations available to an analyst. Averaging
$m$ examples drives $\varepsilon$ to zero, so structure hidden by $\varepsilon$
appears once $m$ is large enough; it never removes $n_y$, which is present in
every example of the class by construction. A linear projection $W^{\!\top} z$
with $W^{\!\top} n_y \approx 0$ does the reverse. We show that clip-level key
geometry is entirely the first case (Sec.~\ref{sec:sample}) and note-level
octave equivalence entirely the second (Sec.~\ref{sec:metric}).

Our contributions are: (i) a three-way measurement that separates musical
information, ambient geometry and readout geometry, with synthetic controls
that make a null interpretable; (ii) evidence that the apparent
``clip-level vs.\ note-level'' contrast in music encoders is a property of the
estimator rather than of temporal context; (iii) a cross-dataset, cross-task
projection that exposes octave equivalence which no amount of averaging
recovers; and (iv) the negative result that this structure is not confined to a
compact subspace, so low-dimensional projection denoises rather than isolates
it.

\section{Measurements}
\label{sec:method}

\noindent\textbf{Representations.} MERT-v1-330M \cite{mert} layers $4$--$24$;
EnCodec 32\,kHz \cite{encodec}, the codec MusicGen consumes \cite{musicgen};
and a log-CQT \cite{brown1991,librosa} as an input floor. A chromagram is the
music-theoretic positive control. Clips are pooled to a single vector by mean
over frames.

\noindent\textbf{Key-centroid geometry.} On GiantSteps \cite{giantsteps}
($7035$ clips, all $24$ keys), we average clip embeddings within key to obtain
$24$ centroids and rank-correlate their pairwise Euclidean distances against
three reference structures: circle-of-fifths distance
$d_5(i,j)=\mathrm{circ}_{12}(7i-7j)$, Krumhansl--Kessler key-profile distance,
and semitone distance $d_{\mathrm{chr}}$. Significance uses a $2000$-draw
permutation null over key labels; $\rho_5 \mid \mathrm{spec}$ additionally
partials out mel-spectral centroid distance. Reporting $d_{\mathrm{chr}}$
alongside $d_5$ is essential---a generic ``nearby keys look alike'' effect would
raise both.

\noindent\textbf{Readout geometry.} The same probe (multinomial logistic
regression, $5$-fold CV) yields $24$ class weight vectors; we apply the identical
measurement to their pairwise cosine distances. This asks whether a linear
readout can reach structure the ambient metric does not express.

\noindent\textbf{Octave equivalence.} On NSynth \cite{nsynth} we use
\emph{real recordings only}---no DSP pitch shifting, which would build the
answer into the stimulus. For each instrument we fix an anchor set of pitches
supporting every transposition $k=1,\dots,25$, so the anchor set is constant
across $k$, and measure $S(k)$, the mean cosine similarity between a note and
its transposition by $k$ semitones. Three statistics are reported together, as
registered in advance:
\begin{align}
\Delta_{12} &= S(12) - \tfrac{1}{2}\big(S(11)+S(13)\big), \\
\Delta_{\mathrm{strict}} &= S(12) - \max_{8 \le k \le 11} S(k),
\end{align}
and $\beta_2$, the coefficient on $\cos(2\pi k/12)$ in a quadratic-plus-periodic
fit of $S(k)$. Confidence intervals are bootstrapped over \emph{instruments}.
$\Delta_{12}$ alone is not sufficient: a positive $\beta_2$ with no local peak
at $k=12$ is a curve-fitting artefact, so we require agreement in sign.

\noindent\textbf{Subspaces.} $W \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times d}$ is an orthonormal
basis of the top-$d$ shrinkage-LDA discriminant directions for a stated target,
$d \le 11$ (any linear map to a $12$-class target has rank $\le 11$). We report
the variance fraction $\mathrm{tr}(W^{\!\top}\Sigma W)/\mathrm{tr}(\Sigma)$ and,
as a control, matched random orthonormal subspaces---projection changes the
metric whatever the directions are.

\section{Information, metric and readout dissociate}
\label{sec:three}

Table~\ref{tab:decodable-vs-geometric} measures all three on the same clips and
keys. The control block sets the scale. An analytic circle of fifths scores
$\approx 1$ in every column, so a null elsewhere is a null of the
representation and not of the method. Random features carry no information and
show nothing. The third control is the load-bearing one: orthogonal per-key
codes decode key \emph{better than any real representation} ($.651$) with
classes equidistant by construction, and their weight geometry is $-0.005$.
In the sense of \cite{controltasks} it is a control task: it holds accuracy
fixed and removes the structure, so what remains in the column is attributable
to the representation. It rules out the natural sceptical reading---that
keys a fifth apart are confusable whatever was fitted, so any accurate $24$-way
classifier ends up with fifths-structured weights.

The dissociation is then unambiguous. The log-CQT has the highest key accuracy
of any real arm and no fifths structure in its centroids ($-0.035$, $p=.52$),
while its probe weights carry that structure strongly ($+0.560$, $z=+9.1$).
Tonal relations are linearly accessible in a log-spectrogram and simply absent
from its metric. Notably, this phenomenon requires no learned encoder at all.

The spectral block also refutes the obvious explanation of why a chromagram
differs from a log-CQT. Neither octave folding nor per-frame normalisation
alone produces fifths geometry ($-0.106$ and $-0.117$ against chroma's
$+0.475$); each alone instead produces \emph{chromatic} structure ($+0.391$,
$+0.491$), the opposite organisation. Only the conjunction yields the circle of
fifths.

What MERT does is move structure between columns rather than create it. Its
readout geometry ($+0.41$ to $+0.48$) is \emph{lower} than that of log-CQT,
EnCodec or chroma, while its centroid geometry is far higher. Across depth,
tonal relational geometry weakens ($\rho_5^{\mathrm{cent}}$: $0.445 \to 0.294$,
L4 to L24) while major/minor separability increases ($0.216 \to 0.328$). We
report this as a correlation: key accuracy does not improve along the way
($.451, .456, .440, .418$), and $\rho_{\mathrm{mode}}$ is $-0.054$ and not
significant in the chromagram control, so the growing mode axis is not
pitch-class based and is at least as consistent with production and
instrumentation in an all-EDM corpus.

\section{Sample-limited masking}
\label{sec:sample}

The clip-level result invites a temporal reading: keys are properties of
seconds of music, so pooling more audio should reveal them. It does not.
Fig.~\ref{fig:averaging}(a) sweeps the pooling window from one frame to the
full $20$\,s clip, drawing one window per clip at every length so the number of
clips behind each centroid is held constant. A single $13$\,ms MERT frame
already yields $\rho_5=+0.276$, $59\%$ of the $20$\,s value, and the chromagram
is flat to within $3\%$. There is no knee and no threshold.

The clip-level measurement averages twice, and only one average was being
varied. Fig.~\ref{fig:averaging}(b) holds the window at the full clip and
sweeps the number of clips per centroid: $+0.035 \to +0.468$ for MERT L12, a
$13.4\times$ change against $1.7\times$ for a $1000\times$ longer window. At
$n=1$---one clip per key, which is the condition the pairwise note-level test
is in---the geometry is indistinguishable from zero for every arm.

This is Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp} with $\varepsilon$ dominant. What differs between
two GiantSteps tracks in the same key---production, instrumentation,
subgenre---is largely unrelated to the key and cancels in the mean. What was
missing at $n=1$ was samples, not audio.

\section{Metric masking}
\label{sec:metric}

If low between-item SNR were the whole story, the same averaging should expose
octave equivalence on NSynth. We pre-registered that prediction and it fails.
Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}(a) sweeps notes per pitch centroid: every arm
saturates by $n=4$ at approximately zero, MERT L24 remains negative, and the
chromagram needs no averaging at all ($\Delta_{\mathrm{strict}}=+0.566$ at
$n=1$). Register and spectral envelope are functions of pitch, so they survive
into every pitch centroid; averaging over instruments cancels the instrument,
never the octave. This is the $n_y$ term.

What removes $n_y$ is a projection, and the evidence is cross-dataset and
cross-task. We fit LDA directions on \emph{GiantSteps track tonics only}---no
notes, no NSynth, no octave information---and apply them unchanged to
individual NSynth recordings with no averaging
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanisms}(b)). At $d=4$, $\Delta_{\mathrm{strict}}$ moves from
$-0.005$ to $+0.223$ for MERT L12, $-0.001$ to $+0.304$ for L4, and $+0.004$ to
$+0.466$ for the log-CQT, with $\Delta_{12}$ and $\beta_2$ agreeing in sign and
bootstrap CIs excluding zero. Matched random subspaces give
$-0.019$ to $+0.007$. The subspace uses $0.11$--$0.24\%$ of MERT's variance and
$1.85\%$ of the log-CQT's.

It would overstate the case to say key supervision \emph{forces} pitch-height
invariance: GiantSteps key correlates with subgenre, tempo and production, and
an LDA may use any of it. The transfer is what licenses the claim, and licenses
a stronger one---structure that survives the move to an independently recorded
isolated-note corpus cannot be solely a GiantSteps-specific nuisance
correlation. The invariance is demonstrated across datasets rather than
guaranteed by the objective. Objectives that build key equivariance in by
construction \cite{stone} are a complementary route we do not pursue here.

\noindent\textbf{A registered prediction that failed.} We predicted that
removing the subspace, $z - W W^{\!\top} z$, would at least halve clip-level
$\rho_5$. Against a baseline recomputed on the same held-out clips it does not
move it (MERT L12: $+0.127 \to +0.119$; L4: $+0.118 \to +0.114$), and only the
$12$-dimensional chromagram collapses, which is a dimensionality effect. Tonal
structure is therefore low-energy and invisible in the ambient metric, but
\emph{not} confined to a compact subspace: many overlapping direction sets carry
it, and a low-dimensional projection denoises a redundantly distributed signal
rather than isolating a unique one.

\section{Discussion}

Three properties that probing literature often treats as one---information
availability, metric visibility, and readout accessibility---are separable, and
separating them changes what a probe result licenses. A high probe accuracy
says $s_y$ is linearly recoverable; it says nothing about whether $s_y$ governs
distances, which is what a downstream model relying on similarity, retrieval or
a nearest-neighbour decision actually consumes.

The two masking modes suggest different remedies. Sample-limited masking is a
property of the evaluation, and the fix is more examples per class, not longer
audio; reporting the number of items behind each centroid should be standard,
since the same encoder scores $+0.035$ or $+0.468$ depending only on that.
Metric masking is a property of the representation, and no evaluation protocol
removes it---only a learned transform does.

Nothing in Eq.~\eqref{eq:decomp} is musical. Semantic relations in vision may be
linearly available while Euclidean geometry is dominated by pose or background,
and phonetic structure in speech may be masked by speaker and channel. The
distinction between information availability and metric visibility plausibly
extends beyond music representations.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} GiantSteps is entirely electronic dance music,
where key correlates with subgenre; the cross-dataset transfer in
Sec.~\ref{sec:metric} constrains that confound but does not remove it, and a
key-balanced, genre-diverse corpus would settle it. NSynth is monophonic and
synthesised or sampled from isolated instruments, so its nuisance structure is
unusually clean. Our subspaces are linear by design; a nonlinear transform
might expose more, at the cost of the interpretability that makes the
$n_y$ account testable. Finally, $\rho_{\mathrm{mode}}$ is reported but not
explained, and we do not claim it is harmonic.

\section{Conclusion}

Musical structure can be present in a representation, linearly accessible, and
still invisible in its geometry---for two different reasons that call for two
different operations. Averaging over examples removes class-independent
nuisance and accounts entirely for clip-level key geometry; a rank-$4$
projection fitted on a different dataset and a different task removes
class-coupled nuisance and recovers note-level octave equivalence that no amount
of averaging reaches. Neither operation creates the structure. Both reveal it.
